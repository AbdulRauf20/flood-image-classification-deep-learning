\begin{abstract}
Deep convolutional networks are routinely compressed by magnitude
pruning before deployment on the resource-constrained platforms used in
disaster response, yet pruning studies are almost always conducted on a
single dataset --- and almost always on optical imagery. We ask a simple
question that this practice leaves unanswered: \emph{does the
compressibility of a CNN transfer across sensing modalities?} We train
ResNet18 and VGG-16 flood classifiers under one fixed supervised
pipeline on two public flood datasets of different modalities ---
FloodNet (optical UAV imagery) and Sen1Floods11 (Sentinel-1 SAR), the
latter converted from per-pixel water masks to image-level labels ---
and prune each model with global unstructured and filter-level
structured $L_1$ magnitude pruning at sparsities from 10\% to 90\%,
fine-tuning every configuration under an identical budget. We find that
\TODO{one-sentence headline result, e.g., unstructured compressibility
transfers across modalities while structured pruning degrades SAR
performance substantially earlier than optical}. Our results suggest
that compression choices validated on optical benchmarks cannot be
assumed to hold for SAR, and we release our protocol to support
multi-seed extensions of this study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Rapid flood mapping is one of the most time-critical applications of
remote sensing: response teams need scene-level situational awareness
within hours of a disaster, often from platforms --- small UAVs, edge
boxes, nanosatellites --- that cannot host large networks
\cite{rahnemoonfar2021floodnet,khan2023floodresnet50}. Model
compression, and in particular magnitude pruning
\cite{han2015learning,li2017pruning}, is the standard route to meeting
such constraints: weights or whole filters with small magnitude are
removed and the network is fine-tuned, frequently recovering accuracy
at high sparsity \cite{blalock2020state}.

The evidence base for this practice, however, is narrow in one
important way. Pruning methods are overwhelmingly validated on optical
(RGB) benchmarks such as ImageNet \cite{deng2009imagenet}, and when
they are applied in remote sensing it is typically to a single dataset
at a time. Flood monitoring in practice spans at least two very
different sensing modalities: high-resolution optical imagery from
UAVs, which is only available in clear conditions, and synthetic
aperture radar (SAR), which penetrates cloud cover and is often the
only usable source during the flood event itself
\cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11}. A deployment pipeline that compresses
a model for the optical case will sooner or later be asked to do the
same for the SAR case.

This paper is built around a single question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Does a heavily pruned classic CNN hold its accuracy consistently
on both optical and SAR flood imagery, or does one modality break down
while the other does not?}
\end{quote}
Either answer is informative. If compressibility transfers, then
sparsity budgets established on optical benchmarks can be reused for
SAR deployments with reasonable confidence. If it does not --- if one
modality degrades earlier or collapses outright --- then the pruning
ratio itself becomes a modality-dependent hyperparameter, and
compression recipes must be re-validated per sensor, which has direct
consequences for operational flood-mapping systems.

Answering the question fairly requires removing every confound except
the data. We therefore fix a single supervised pipeline --- identical
architecture definitions, input resolution, augmentation, class
balancing, optimizer, schedule, checkpoint selection, pruning
operators, sparsity levels, and fine-tuning budget --- and vary
\emph{only} the dataset. We instantiate the study with two standard
backbones of very different capacity, ResNet18 (11.2M parameters)
\cite{he2016resnet} and VGG-16 (134.3M parameters)
\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, on two public flood-classification datasets:
FloodNet \cite{rahnemoonfar2021floodnet}, optical UAV imagery from
Hurricane Harvey, and Sen1Floods11 \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11},
Sentinel-1 SAR chips of global flood events. Sen1Floods11 is labeled
per pixel, so we convert its hand-labeled water masks into image-level
flooded/non-flooded labels with an explicit, reproducible rule
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Each dense model is then pruned with the two
canonical magnitude-pruning families --- global unstructured $L_1$
pruning \cite{han2015learning} and structured $L_1$ filter pruning
\cite{li2017pruning} --- at five ratios from 10\% to 90\%, and every
pruned model is fine-tuned under the same budget as its dense parent.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled $2\times2\times2\times5$ study (two architectures,
two modalities, two pruning families, five sparsity ratios; 44 trained
models in total) in which the training pipeline is held fixed and only
the dataset varies, isolating the effect of sensing modality on
compressibility.
\item A reproducible protocol for converting per-pixel SAR flood masks
(Sen1Floods11) into image-level classification labels, enabling
like-for-like comparison with image-level optical benchmarks
(FloodNet).

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Flood Classification Datasets and Baselines}
\textbf{FloodNet} \cite{rahnemoonfar2021floodnet} is a high-resolution
($3000\times4000$) UAV imagery dataset collected with DJI Mavic Pro
quadcopters after Hurricane Harvey, providing a semi-supervised Track-1
challenge with 398 labeled training images (51 flooded, 347
non-flooded), 1{,}047 unlabeled training images, and validation/test
splits whose classification labels were withheld for the challenge
evaluation. \textbf{Sen1Floods11} \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11}
provides 4{,}831 Sentinel-1 SAR chips of $512\times512$ pixels covering
11 global flood events, including 446 hand-labeled chips whose
per-pixel masks distinguish water, non-water, and invalid pixels; it is
designed for per-pixel flood segmentation rather than image-level
classification, which motivates the label conversion we describe in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

On the modeling side, Khose \emph{et al.}
\cite{khose2021semisupervised} established a strong FloodNet Track-1
classification baseline: a ResNet18 trained with pseudo-label
semi-supervision \cite{lee2013pseudo} reaching 96.70\% test accuracy
with 11.6M parameters --- outperforming substantially larger
InceptionV3, ResNet50 and Xception baselines. Their result motivates
our choice of ResNet18 as the small reference backbone; in this work,
however, we deliberately use \emph{purely supervised} training for all
runs, since pseudo-labeling would entangle compression effects with
semi-supervision effects. Closest to our deployment motivation, Khan
\emph{et al.} \cite{khan2023floodresnet50} propose Flood-ResNet50, a
modified ResNet50 for flooded/non-flooded UAV image classification
evaluated on-device (Jetson Nano) with 96.43\% accuracy and 86.36\% F1
on FloodNet. Their study targets efficiency via architecture design on
a single optical dataset; ours is complementary, asking whether a
\emph{given} architecture compressed by pruning behaves consistently
across optical and SAR data.

\subsection{Network Pruning}
Magnitude pruning removes parameters deemed unimportant by weight
magnitude. \emph{Unstructured} pruning \cite{han2015learning} zeroes
individual weights globally across layers and achieves high sparsity
with little accuracy loss, though realized speedups require sparse
kernels. \emph{Structured} pruning \cite{li2017pruning} removes entire
filters or neurons, producing dense sub-networks that accelerate on
commodity hardware but constraining what can be removed. Subsequent
work has questioned what pruning actually preserves: the lottery-ticket
hypothesis \cite{frankle2019lottery} locates sparse trainable
sub-networks, Liu \emph{et al.} \cite{liu2019rethinking} argue that
the value of pruning lies largely in the architecture it induces, and
Blalock \emph{et al.} \cite{blalock2020state} document that pruning
papers overwhelmingly evaluate on a small set of optical benchmarks
under inconsistent protocols. To our knowledge, no prior work has held
a flood-classification pipeline fixed and measured how the
\emph{same} pruning protocol behaves across an optical and a SAR
dataset; this is the gap we address.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Label Conversion}
\textbf{FloodNet (optical).} We use the 398 labeled Track-1 training
images (51 flooded / 347 non-flooded). Because classification labels
for the official validation and test splits were withheld for the
challenge server, all labeled evaluation must come from these 398
images: we use a stratified 50/50 split (199 train / 199 validation,
fixed seed), mirroring the split strategy of the public baseline
implementation \cite{khose2021semisupervised}. Images are resized from
$3000\times4000$ to $400\times300$.

\textbf{Sen1Floods11 (SAR).} We use the hand-labeled subset: Sentinel-1
chips with two polarization bands (VV, VH) and per-pixel masks with
values $\{1{=}\text{water},\,0{=}\text{non-water},\,-1{=}\text{invalid}\}$.
Since the dataset is natively per-pixel, we convert each chip to an
image-level label with the rule
\begin{equation}
y = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\frac{\left|\{p : m_p = 1\}\right|}
{\left|\{p : m_p \neq -1\}\right|} \;\geq\; \tau\right],
\qquad \tau = 0.01,
\label{eq:labelconv}
\end{equation}
i.e., a chip is \emph{flooded} if at least 1\% of its valid pixels are
water; chips with no valid pixels are discarded. This yields 441
usable chips (293 flooded / 148 non-flooded), split 50/50 stratified
into 220 train / 221 validation chips with the same seed as FloodNet.
For input compatibility with ImageNet-pretrained backbones, SAR
backscatter is clipped to $[-50, 1]$\,dB, min--max scaled to $[0,1]$,
and stacked as three channels $(\mathrm{VV}, \mathrm{VH},
(\mathrm{VV}{+}\mathrm{VH})/2)$, then bilinearly resized to
$400\times300$.

Note the two datasets are imbalanced in \emph{opposite} directions:
flooded images are the minority class in FloodNet (12.8\%) but the
majority class in Sen1Floods11 (66.4\%). We return to the evaluation
consequences of this in Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Unified Training Pipeline}
All dense baselines and all fine-tuning runs share one pipeline,
identical across datasets and architectures: ImageNet-pretrained
backbone \cite{deng2009imagenet} with the final layer replaced by a
2-logit head; cross-entropy loss; random horizontal and vertical flips
as the only augmentation; a class-balanced weighted random sampler;
SGD with learning rate $0.01$ and no momentum; 50 epochs; and model
selection by best validation F1. The only architecture-forced
difference is the batch size (64 for ResNet18, 16 for VGG-16, due to
the memory footprint of VGG-16 at $400\times300$); it is held constant
across datasets so that every optical-vs-SAR comparison is batch-size
matched. All runs use a single fixed seed (42) for the split, the
initialization and the data order (see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Pruning Protocol}
Starting from each dense checkpoint we apply, separately:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Global unstructured $L_1$} \cite{han2015learning}:
individual weights of all convolutional and linear layers are ranked
globally by magnitude and the smallest fraction $r$ is zeroed.
\item \textbf{Structured $L_1$} \cite{li2017pruning}: for every
convolutional layer with more than 8 output channels, the fraction $r$
of output filters with smallest $L_1$ norm is removed (masked); for
every linear layer with more than 8 output units, the same rule is
applied to rows. The final 2-unit classifier is never pruned.
\end{itemize}
with $r \in \{0.10, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.90\}$. Each pruned model is
fine-tuned for 50 epochs --- the same budget, optimizer and learning
rate as dense training --- and the best-validation-F1 checkpoint is
reported. Sparsity masks are kept fixed during fine-tuning. For every
configuration we report the number of nonzero parameters and the
effective multiply--accumulate count of the surviving weights. In
total the study comprises $2$ architectures $\times$ $2$ datasets
$\times$ ($1$ dense $+$ $2$ methods $\times$ $5$ ratios) $= 44$
trained models.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experiments}
We run the full protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup} on all four
architecture--dataset pairs. For each pair we track validation
accuracy, F1, precision, recall and ROC-AUC at every epoch and report
the best-F1 checkpoint per configuration. We additionally monitor two
failure signatures: (i) \emph{collapse} to a constant majority-class
predictor, identified by recall $=1.0$ jointly with AUC $\approx 0.5$,
and (ii) silent degradation, identified by falling AUC at stable F1.
Dense checkpoints are trained once per pair and shared by all pruning
configurations of that pair, so that every pruned model of a pair
starts from identical weights.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Single seed.} All 44 models share one seed; differences smaller
than seed noise cannot be distinguished. Multi-seed replication of at
least the structured-pruning runs is the most important extension, and
our earlier multi-seed experience on Sen1Floods11 suggests seed
variance is non-negligible at small validation sizes.

\textbf{Small evaluation sets.} The withheld FloodNet val/test labels
force evaluation on 199 held-out images (25 flooded); Sen1Floods11
evaluation uses 221 chips. Single-image changes move FloodNet F1 by
roughly 2 points, so small deltas must be interpreted cautiously.

\textbf{Label conversion threshold.} The image-level labels for
Sen1Floods11 depend on the $\tau=1\%$ water-fraction rule of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:labelconv}; borderline chips change class under other
thresholds, and a sensitivity analysis over $\tau$ is left to future
work.

\textbf{Masked rather than physically removed structure.} Structured
pruning is applied as weight masks; channels are zeroed but not
excised, so reported FLOP reductions are theoretical. Similarly,
unstructured sparsity yields no wall-clock speedup without sparse
kernels. Neither affects the accuracy comparison, which is our focus.

\textbf{Modality is not the only difference.} FloodNet and
Sen1Floods11 differ in resolution, scene content, geography, and label
provenance (direct annotation vs.\ mask-derived labels), in addition
to sensor physics. Our design holds the pipeline fixed but cannot
attribute the observed gap to sensor modality alone; disentangling
these factors (e.g., with paired optical/SAR acquisitions) is future
work.

\textbf{Opposite class imbalance.} Because the flooded class is the
FloodNet minority but the Sen1Floods11 majority, aggregate metrics are
not directly comparable across datasets; we therefore compare each
pruned model only to its own dense baseline, never across datasets in
absolute terms.

\textbf{ImageNet pretraining for SAR.} Both backbones start from
RGB-pretrained weights, which is standard but favorable to the optical
dataset; SAR-specific pretraining might change where structured
pruning begins to hurt.
